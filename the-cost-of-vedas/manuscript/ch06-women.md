# Chapter 6 — Women and Gender

## The priest who would not look at her

At her father's cremation, the eldest of three daughters was told she could not
light the pyre. There was no son. A male cousin was located, flown in, and
handed the torch by a priest who addressed every instruction to him and none to
her.

She had paid for the funeral. She had nursed her father for two years. She had
read more of the tradition than the cousin ever would.

Afterwards, looking for the rule that had excluded her, she could not find it.
Not because it is hidden — because in the oldest layer of the corpus, it is not
there.

## The name that survived

In the Rigveda, a woman speaks and the text keeps her words.

**Lopamudra** argues with her husband, the aged sage Agastya, in **Rigveda
1.179**. She wants him; he has withdrawn into austerity. The tradition assigns
the opening verses to her — and the hymn preserves her side of the argument
rather than only his reply. **Apala** appears in **Rigveda 8.91**, afflicted and
seeking a cure. **Ghosha** is named as the seer of **10.39 and 10.40**.
**Vishvavara**, **Romasha**, **Yami** — the tradition names them too.

One clarification is owed at the outset, because the rest of this book depends
on the reader trusting its citations. The Rigveda's hymns do not sign
themselves. The attribution of each hymn to a named seer comes from the
**Anukramaṇī**, an index compiled centuries after the hymns themselves. So the
honest claim is not *"Lopamudra wrote this"* — we cannot know that. The honest
claim is that **the tradition that preserved these hymns chose to record women
as their seers**, and kept doing so for three thousand years.

That is a weaker claim than the one usually made. It is also the more
remarkable one. The men who transmitted this corpus syllable by syllable, who
had every opportunity across a hundred generations to quietly reassign those
hymns to male authors, did not. Whatever came later in this chapter, that
belongs at the start of it.

## What the texts say

The Rigveda is not one book. It is a library assembled over centuries, and its
layers do not agree with each other.

In the earliest layer, a woman may speak in the ritual assembly. The marriage
hymn — **Rigveda 10.85**, the Sūryā Sūkta — addresses the bride directly and
speaks of her entering the household as its mistress. She is a participant in
the rite, not its object.

By the time of the later Vedic texts and the Dharmaśāstra literature that
follows, the position has narrowed. Ritual roles that were open are closed.
Inheritance rights that existed become conditional. The right to study the
Veda, which the early texts do not obviously bar, is explicitly restricted.

Nothing in the texts explains this change. Scholars disagree sharply about why
it happened — whether it reflects settlement and property, contact and
conflict, or the ordinary hardening of any institution as it ages. **This book
takes no side on that question.** It only insists that the change is visible,
and that it runs in one direction.

## The gift

What the Vedic corpus gave women is not nothing, and it is not what either
side of the argument usually claims.

It gave **named female authorship in a sacred canon** — rarer in the ancient
world than modern readers assume. It gave the category of the woman as a seer,
one who sees truth directly rather than receiving it through a man. And it gave
a textual record that later reformers could point to when they argued for
change, because the argument *"your own oldest texts say otherwise"* is the
strongest one available inside a tradition.

Every Indian reform movement that expanded women's education — from the
nineteenth century onward — used exactly this move.

## The cost

The cost is equally real, and stating it gently would be dishonest.

A canon that names women seers and then, in its later layers, restricts women's
access to that same canon creates a **permanent instrument of exclusion**. The
restriction carries the authority of the whole corpus, while the earlier
openness is treated as a curiosity.

Second, interpretation passed almost entirely into male hands for most of
recorded history. The commentaries that shaped how the Vedas were *read* — and
therefore how they were lived — were written by men, for a male audience of
students. A text may be generous; a commentary can narrow it without altering
a syllable.

Third, and most damaging: selective quotation. A tradition with layers that
disagree gives every faction a verse. Those who wished to restrict women found
their verse. Those who wished to liberate found theirs. **The text became a
quarry rather than a guide.**

And the cost compounds, because exclusion from the text meant exclusion from
the argument about the text. A woman barred from Sanskrit could not check
whether the rule excluding her existed. The daughter at the cremation is
unusual only in that she went and looked. Most people never do, which is
precisely what the arrangement depended on.

The fourth cost is the one with a body count. The purity apparatus of Chapter 2
attached itself to women's bodies with particular force: menstrual seclusion,
post-partum isolation, the exclusion of women from temples and kitchens on
specific days. Some of this persists. Women have died in menstrual huts in
South Asia within the last decade. None of it is required by anything in the
Rigveda. All of it has been defended as Vedic.

## What remains

In the diaspora, something unexpected has happened.

Communities separated from the enforcing structures of village custom — in
Mauritius, in Trinidad, in Fiji, in Britain and North America — often read the
texts without the apparatus of local caste and gender policing that surrounded
them at home. The text travelled; much of the enforcement did not.

The result is a reading public encountering Ghosha and Lopamudra with fresh
eyes, and asking a question that is difficult to answer from within the
tradition: *if the oldest layer named women as seers, on whose authority was
that stopped?*

That question is the most valuable thing this dimension has produced. It was
made possible by the very preservation that also carried the restriction. Both
arrived in the same box.

The daughter at the cremation eventually found what she was looking for, which
was an absence. No hymn forbids her. The prohibition that governed the most
important hour of her life was assembled later, by people, and attributed to a
text that does not contain it.

She lit a lamp at home the following week, alone, using a verse from the tenth
mandala. Whether that counts is a question the tradition has never satisfactorily
answered — because the people best placed to answer it were, for most of
recorded history, the people forbidden to ask.
