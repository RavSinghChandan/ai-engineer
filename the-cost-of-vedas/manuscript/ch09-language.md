# Chapter 9 — Language and Expression

## Three languages, one person

He thinks in English. He argues with his mother in Bhojpuri. He prays in
Sanskrit, a language he cannot speak, using sounds he learned before he learned
what they meant.

None of these is fully his, and the arrangement is so ordinary in India that it
takes a foreigner to find it strange.

## What the texts say

Vedic Sanskrit is the oldest substantially attested Indo-European language, and
its documentation was a deliberate scientific project rather than an accident of
survival.

The tradition treated language itself as an object of study. The *śikṣā*
phonetics specified how each sound is produced: the place of articulation, the
effort, the voicing, the pitch. *Vyākaraṇa* — grammar — culminated in **Pāṇini's
Aṣṭādhyāyī**, roughly the fourth century BCE, which describes Sanskrit in about
four thousand rules organised so that specific rules override general ones.

That last detail is why linguists still discuss it. The rule-ordering system
Pāṇini used anticipates the structure of formal grammars in a way that was
noticed independently when generative linguistics developed in the twentieth
century, and his work was a direct influence on it. A grammar written before
Alexander reached India remains, by common assessment, among the most complete
descriptions of any language ever produced.

The corpus also treats sound as **efficacious rather than merely referential**.
The mantra works through its sound, correctly produced, not only through its
meaning. This is why the phonetics had to be exact, and why the transmission
system of Chapter 8 preserved pitch accent — in a Vedic recitation, a wrong
accent is a wrong word.

## The gift

**A language engineered for precision.** Sanskrit's morphology allows relations
between ideas to be marked on the words themselves rather than by position, and
its compounding permits dense construction of technical vocabulary. This is why
it became the working language of Indian mathematics, astronomy, medicine,
logic, law and philosophy for well over a millennium.

**Linguistics as a discipline, founded here.** Pāṇini's descriptive method —
observe the language as used, derive the rules that generate it, formalise them
— is the method modern linguistics uses. The tradition produced the first
rigorous grammar of any language anywhere, and produced it early.

**The comparative discovery that reorganised European scholarship.** When
European scholars encountered Sanskrit in the eighteenth century, the
resemblances to Greek and Latin forced the hypothesis of a common ancestor.
Comparative philology, and with it the modern understanding of how languages
descend and diverge, began from that encounter.

**A vast literature of extraordinary range.** Not only the sacred corpus: poetry,
drama, mathematics, erotics, statecraft, medicine, aesthetics. Kālidāsa, the
*Arthaśāstra*, Bhāskara, the dramatic theory of the *Nāṭyaśāstra*. A working
intellectual language, not a liturgical one only.

## The cost

**A sacred language is a restricted language.** Everything in Chapters 6, 7 and
8 was enforced through access to Sanskrit. If the authoritative texts, the law,
the medicine, the philosophy and the ritual are all in one language, and that
language is taught only to some, then those excluded cannot participate in any
of it — nor verify what is being claimed on their behalf. The gate was
linguistic, and it held for two and a half thousand years.

**The prestige hierarchy damaged the living languages.** Sanskrit's status as
the language of the gods positioned Prakrits, and later Bhojpuri, Awadhi,
Maithili, Tamil and dozens of others, as lesser — fit for ordinary life, not for
serious thought. That ranking is still audible in Indian classrooms and drawing
rooms. The man arguing with his mother in Bhojpuri has absorbed, without ever
being told, that his argument would be more serious in English.

**Sound over sense was a defensible doctrine with an indefensible outcome.**
Reciting correctly without understanding is, in its own terms, coherent. In
practice it produced a population performing a text it could not read,
dependent on interpreters, unable to notice when what was attributed to the text
was not in it. Chapter 7 is the direct consequence.

**And the replacement gate is English.** Colonial administration installed a
second linguistic hierarchy on top of the first, and independence did not remove
it. Access to the professions, the courts, and the better jobs runs through
English. The tradition's inherited assumption that serious matters happen in a
language most people do not speak was not dismantled. It was re-pointed at a
different language and left running.

## What remains

The diaspora is where this dimension's losses are most visible and most
measurable, because they happen on a schedule.

The pattern is consistent across Britain, North America, the Gulf and East
Africa: the first generation speaks the mother tongue fluently; the second
understands it and answers in English; the third does not understand it. Three
generations from immigration to loss, with individual families varying but the
trend robust.

What survives longest is the least semantic material. A grandchild who cannot
hold a conversation in Gujarati will still know the *Gāyatrī*, because the
*Gāyatrī* was never transmitted as meaning — it was transmitted as sound, which
is exactly what the tradition designed it to be. The doctrine that drew the
sharpest criticism in the previous section is the reason anything survives at
all.

There is also a genuine and underexamined gain. English gave the Indian
diaspora a global professional language and gave Indians internally a link
language that belongs to no single region — which is why it survived
independence. It carries a colonial cost that is real. It also carries an
Indian literature now read worldwide.

Three languages, none fully his. It is worth saying plainly that this is not
only a loss. He can pray in a language older than Homer, argue in the language
his family has spoken for centuries, and work in the language of the world's
laboratories. Most people get one.

What he cannot do is what his great-grandfather could: understand the prayer.
