# Chapter 3 — Finance and Wealth

## The question nobody asks out loud

A software engineer in Bengaluru earns more at twenty-eight than her father did
at fifty-eight. She has a portfolio, a term plan, an emergency fund of six
months' expenses, and a spreadsheet that projects her retirement corpus to the
year 2061.

She also has a persistent, low-grade guilt about wanting more, and no framework
for deciding when to stop.

Her grandmother would have recognised neither the spreadsheet nor the guilt as
a problem. She would have recognised the question underneath it, because the
tradition she was raised in asked it directly: **what is a human being allowed
to want?**

That question has a Vedic answer. It is more permissive than most people
assume, and the way it was later narrowed is one of the more consequential
costs in this book.

## What the texts say

Start with the thing that surprises people.

**The early Vedic corpus is not ascetic.** It is frankly, unembarrassedly
prosperous. The hymns ask for cattle, for horses, for strong sons, for rain,
for victory, for long life, for wealth. There is no suggestion that wanting
these things is spiritually inferior to not wanting them. A civilisation
producing the Rigveda was a civilisation that considered material flourishing a
legitimate thing to pray for.

Against that, **Rigveda 10.117** — the hymn on liberality — sets a limit, and
sets it memorably. In Griffith's translation: *the riches of the liberal never
waste away, while he who will not give finds none to comfort him.* The hymn
observes that the gods have not ordained hunger as our death, that death comes
in many shapes even to the well-fed man, and that the one who feeds the beggar
who comes to him in need makes a friend for future troubles.

Read that carefully, because it is doing something precise. It does not say
wealth is bad. It says **wealth that does not circulate is not wealth** — it is
a stock awaiting loss. The argument is social and almost economic: generosity
is presented as the mechanism by which prosperity is made durable.

Then comes the framework everyone has heard of and most people date wrongly.

The *puruṣārthas* — the aims of a human life — are usually recited as four:
*dharma* (right conduct), *artha* (prosperity), *kāma* (pleasure and desire),
*mokṣa* (liberation). But the earliest Vedic layers work with **three**, the
*trivarga*: dharma, artha, kāma. **Mokṣa is the late arrival**, emerging with
the Upaniṣads around the middle of the first millennium BCE as the philosophy
of renunciation developed. The comprehensive fourfold scheme is first set out
not in the Vedas at all but in the Dharmaśāstra literature and the epics.

This matters enormously for the argument of this chapter. The original scheme
of human aims **contained no exit door.** Prosperity and pleasure sat alongside
right conduct as legitimate ends of a life — not as concessions to weakness,
not as lower rungs, but as aims. A person pursuing wealth was pursuing one of
the three things a human being is for.

Renunciation was added on top. And once added, it did not stay on top — it went
to the front.

## The gift

Three gifts here, and the first is the one modern readers most need.

**Wanting was licensed.** A tradition that lists prosperity among the proper
aims of a human life gives its inheritors permission to build, earn, trade and
accumulate without spiritual apology. Much of the entrepreneurial energy of
Indian trading communities — Gujarati, Marwari, Chettiar, Sindhi — operated
inside a framework where commerce was not a fall from grace. Compare this with
traditions that had to argue their way out of a prohibition on lending at
interest, and the advantage is visible in the historical record.

**Circulation was built into the definition of wealth.** Rigveda 10.117's
insight — that hoarded wealth is fragile and given wealth is durable — became
*dāna*, a structural obligation rather than an optional virtue. The temple
economy, the *dharamshala*, the community kitchen, the endowed water tank, the
merchant who funds a school: these are not charity in the modern sense of
surplus disposed of at year end. They are the tax the framework levies on
success, and Indian philanthropy still runs at rates that puzzle observers who
expect it to track Western patterns.

**A stopping rule was offered.** The fourfold scheme, whatever its late
assembly, gives a life a shape: build in the middle, release at the end. A
person who knows the accumulation phase has a terminus is protected from the
specific modern disease of accumulating with no idea what the number is for.
Our Bengaluru engineer has a spreadsheet running to 2061 and no answer to
*then what*. The framework at least had an answer.

## The cost

The costs are large, and two of them are still being paid.

**Renunciation acquired a prestige that production never matched.** Once mokṣa
was added and elevated, the person who withdrew outranked the person who built.
This is a strange arrangement for any society, and it produced a durable
asymmetry: the merchant funded the monastery, the monastery conferred the
status, and the merchant's own work remained one rung down no matter how much
good it did. A civilisation that systematically undervalues its producers
relative to its renouncers will, over enough centuries, have fewer producers
than it needs.

**The framework was fused to caste, and the fusion was catastrophic.** In the
scheme of Chapter 7, trade and production belong to a specific varṇa. What began
as a legitimate human aim available to anyone became an assigned function
belonging to some. Prosperity was still licensed — but licensed *by birth*. The
Vedic permission to want became, in practice, a permission extended to those
already permitted everything else. Millions were locked out of the artha they
were philosophically entitled to pursue, by a hierarchy resting on a single
late verse.

**Fatalism did real financial damage.** *Karma*, which is not itself a Vedic
Saṃhitā doctrine but became fused with this framework, supplied an explanation
for poverty that removed the obligation to fix it. If a person's circumstances
are earned, then both the poor man's suffering and the rich man's comfort are
just. This has been used, and is still used, to blunt redistribution,
resist land reform, and explain away destitution as accounting rather than
injustice. The texts do not require that reading. It was drawn anyway, and it
was convenient for precisely the people who drew it.

**And the stopping rule stopped working.** The fourfold life-stage scheme
assumed a village, an extended family that absorbs the retiring generation, and
a life expectancy around fifty. None of those hold. A modern Indian
professional gets the guilt about wanting — the residue of renunciation's
prestige — without the structure that made release survivable. That is the
worst of both systems: the anxiety of the old framework, the insecurity of the
new one.

## What remains

In the diaspora this dimension produced the tradition's most visible worldly
success, and its most visible internal conflict.

Indian-origin communities in the United States, Britain, East Africa, the Gulf
and South East Asia are disproportionately represented in medicine, technology,
trade and the professions. Some of that is selection — migration filters for
the determined. But a framework that licensed accumulation without spiritual
apology, and that bound success to visible communal giving, travels well into a
market economy. The Indian-origin entrepreneur who funds a hospital wing is
doing something his great-grandfather would have recognised immediately.

What has not travelled is the stopping rule. The Bengaluru engineer's problem is
now the Edison and Leicester and Nairobi problem too: the accumulation phase
runs, the *dāna* obligation is honoured, and there is no third act, because the
third act required a village that no longer exists.

And in its place, something the texts would have found unrecognisable — the
spiritualisation of the accumulation itself. Prosperity gospels dressed in
Sanskrit, wealth as evidence of merit, the *puruṣārtha* framework quoted to
justify exactly the unbounded acquisition it was designed to bound.

The Vedas licensed wanting. They did not license wanting without end. That
distinction survived three thousand years of transmission and was lost in
roughly one generation of prosperity.
