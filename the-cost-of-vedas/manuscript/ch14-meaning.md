# Chapter 14 — Meaning and Mortality

## The hymn that admits it does not know

A man in his fifties sits with his father's death certificate and finds that
the question he cannot answer is not *where has he gone* but *what was any of
it for*.

He is not religious. He would describe himself, if asked, as someone who
respects the tradition without believing it. He has no framework for the
question and no confidence that one exists.

There is a hymn for precisely this, and its remarkable quality is that it
refuses to comfort him.

## What the texts say

**Rigveda 10.129**, the *Nāsadīya Sūkta*, should not exist.

It asks how everything began, and having asked, it declines to answer. It
begins before the categories: neither being nor non-being, no air, no sky, no
death, no immortality, no day or night. Something stirs; desire arises. And
then, where every scripture on earth places its certainty, this hymn places a
question — *who really knows? who can here declare it? whence was it born,
whence this creation?* The gods, it notes, came **after** the world was made. So
how would they know?

And then the final line, which is among the most extraordinary sentences in
religious literature: the one who surveys it from the highest heaven — **he
alone knows, or perhaps he does not know.**

*Or perhaps he does not know.*

A tradition put that at the summit of its most protected text and then memorised
it, syllable by syllable, for three thousand years.

Note where it sits. Mandala 10 is the latest layer of the Rigveda — the same
layer that produced the single verse on which Chapter 7's hierarchy was built.
**The corpus's final stratum gave humanity both the verse used to justify
hereditary rank and the hymn that refuses to claim knowledge of ultimate
things.** It did not resolve the tension. It kept both.

That is the central fact of this book.

On death specifically, the early material is less metaphysical than readers
expect. **Rigveda 10.18** is a funeral hymn and is mostly concerned with the
living: let the living remain with the living, it says, turning the mourners
back toward life. The elaborate machinery of rebirth and liberation belongs to
the Upaniṣadic layer and after — *saṃsāra*, *karma*, *mokṣa* — and as Chapter 3
noted, *mokṣa* is a late arrival in the scheme of human aims.

The Upaniṣads' answer to the man with the death certificate is identity: *tat
tvam asi*, that thou art. The self at its deepest is not similar to the ground
of being; it is that ground. Death then becomes a change in the arrangement of
something that was never separate.

One honest caution. The Upaniṣads do not speak with one voice, and the coherent
system usually presented is largely the work of later commentators — Śaṅkara
above all, in the eighth century CE, read back into texts composed more than a
millennium earlier. The texts themselves are more various and stranger than the
philosophy extracted from them.

## The gift

**Doubt licensed from inside scripture, permanently.** This is the gift that
makes every other one in this book possible. A tradition whose most sacred text
contains *perhaps he does not know* can never credibly treat questioning as
foreign to itself. Every later Indian sceptic, materialist and logician argued
from within a tradition that had already conceded the limits of knowledge in its
own foundation. That licence cannot be revoked without deleting a hymn from the
Veda.

**A death that is not a catastrophe.** Placing the individual inside a larger
process — ancestral, cyclical, or metaphysical — makes mortality bearable
without requiring anyone to believe anything specific about an afterlife. The
*pitṛ* rites of Chapter 5 do this socially; the Upaniṣadic identity claim does
it philosophically. Both work by making the individual non-central.

**Meaning available without belief.** The man with the death certificate does
not have to accept any proposition to use 10.129. The hymn's offer is that the
question is legitimate, ancient, and unanswered by anyone — which is a form of
company, and is more than most frameworks give him.

**Philosophy of mind conducted as inquiry.** The Upaniṣadic method — first-person
investigation, argued and contested rather than revealed — produced the material
that Chapter 1 traced into modern clinical practice.

## The cost

**Interiority became a reason to accept the exterior.** If the deepest self is
untouched and identical with the ground of being, then hunger, exclusion and the
well one may not draw from can be framed as belonging to a lower order of
reality. The philosophy does not require that conclusion. It makes it available,
it was drawn, and it functioned as an anaesthetic. Chapter 7's hierarchy and this
chapter's metaphysics were never sealed off from each other.

**Karma turned suffering into accounting.** A framework in which circumstances
are earned supplies an explanation for another person's misery that dissolves the
obligation to relieve it. This is the single most damaging idea in this book's
subject matter, and it is not a Saṃhitā doctrine — it developed later and fused
with the hierarchy. It is still used to explain destitution as justice.

**Renunciation outranked everything.** As Chapters 3 and 4 found, the person who
withdrew outranked the person who built, fed and paid. A civilisation that
ranks its producers below its renouncers, over enough centuries, gets fewer
producers than it needs.

**And depth became a shield.** This one is live. The genuine sophistication of
10.129 is now routinely deployed to deflect criticism of anything done in the
tradition's name — as though the magnificence of one hymn settles what another
was used to do. It does not. Both are in the book. That is the point of this
book.

## What remains

The *Nāsadīya* has escaped. It appears in physics lectures, in poetry, in the
epigraphs of books whose authors have read nothing else of the Rigveda. It
travels because it requires no belief to be admired: an ancient text staring at
the origin of everything and refusing to pretend it knows is legible to a
sceptic in any century.

In the diaspora this dimension has fared better than any other in this book,
because it is the part of the inheritance that loses nothing in translation.
Caste needed a village. Ritual needed fire, materials, a priest. But a question
about the nature of the self needs nothing but someone willing to sit with it,
and it can be asked in English at a kitchen table in Leicester.

What was lost in transit is the rigour. The six schools are largely gone from
popular transmission; what circulates is a soft residue — *all is one*, *the self
is divine* — without the ferocious argumentative discipline that produced it.
The tradition's philosophy is now widely admired and rarely studied.

Fourteen dimensions, and a pattern has held across every one of them.

In each, the gift is in the oldest layer and the cost is in what was added
later and attributed backwards. The hierarchy: one late verse. The exclusion of
women: not in the hymns that name them as seers. The sea-crossing ban: absent
from a corpus full of journeys. The birth-fixed occupation: contradicted by
Rigveda 9.112. The prestige of renunciation: an addition to a scheme of human
aims that originally had three.

Every restriction examined in this book required a village to enforce it and a
gate to keep people from checking. The diaspora removed the village. Literacy
removed the gate.

And what is left, when both are gone, is a corpus that preserved — with a
fidelity nothing else in human history matches — the evidence needed to examine
what was done in its name. Chapter 8's checksums did not only protect the
hymns. They protected the counts, the layers and the diction that let a reader
in 2026 establish that *śūdra* appears exactly once.

If I am asked to name the single most valuable thing the four Vedas gave the
world, I do not hesitate, and it is not a doctrine.

It is a sentence: **or perhaps he does not know.**

A tradition that preserved its own uncertainty at the highest point of its most
sacred text handed every future generation permission to keep asking. That is
why a person raised inside it can catalogue the damage honestly and still
belong.

The Vedas never claimed to have finished. Everything in this book that went
wrong went wrong when someone insisted they had.
