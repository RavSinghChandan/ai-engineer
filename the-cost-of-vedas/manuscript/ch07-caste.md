# Chapter 7 — Social Order and Caste

## One verse

Almost everything the world believes the Vedas say about caste rests on a
single verse.

It is **Rigveda 10.90.12**, part of the hymn called the **Puruṣa Sūkta**. The
hymn describes a cosmic being sacrificed so that the universe may be made from
his body. In verse 12, the social order emerges from that body: the brahmin
from his mouth, the warrior from his arms, the producer from his thighs, the
śūdra from his feet.

Four groups. One body. A hierarchy with the authority of creation itself
behind it.

That verse has been used for perhaps two and a half thousand years to justify
who may enter a temple, who may draw water from which well, who may marry
whom, who may read, who may hold land, and who must handle the dead. Millions
of lives were narrowed by it. Some were ended by it.

So it matters, more than almost any other question in this book, what that
verse actually is.

## What the texts say

Here is what can be established, and it is not a small thing.

**The Puruṣa Sūkta is the only hymn in the entire Rigveda that names the four
varṇas explicitly.** More than a thousand hymns, ten books, composed across
centuries — and the fourfold social order appears once.

**The word śūdra occurs nowhere else in the Rigveda.** Not in the family books,
not in the battle hymns, not in the ritual instructions. Only here, in a single
verse, in the last layer of the text.

And that layer is the crux. **Mandala 10, where this hymn sits, is the latest
part of the Rigveda** — later than the core family books by a wide margin, on
this both Indian and Western scholarship agree. The hymn's language gives it
away: as far back as 1868, John Muir observed that the varṇa verse has *"every
character of modernness both in its diction and ideas"* compared to the archaic
Vedic around it. Later scholars have read the same signal — a composition whose
vocabulary and style belong to a later world than the hymns it was filed
beside.

The standard scholarly reading follows from that. The fourfold hierarchy was
not the seed from which Vedic society grew. **It was added near the end, to give
religious sanction to inequality that had already emerged** in the Kuru polity —
a settled, stratified, unequal society reaching back into its most sacred text
to have its arrangements blessed.

I want to be careful here, because this is the point at which a book like this
can overclaim. The late dating of the hymn is well supported. But that it was
*inserted specifically to legitimise an existing hierarchy* is an inference —
strong, widely held, and not directly provable from the text. The text shows us
the verse is late and isolated. It does not hand us the motive. I say this not
to soften the finding but because a reader who later discovers I blurred that
line will stop trusting the rest of the book.

What the early hymns give instead is far less orderly. Occupation appears as
something done rather than something inherited. **Rigveda 9.112** — a short,
almost comic hymn — has a poet, a physician and a grinder of corn each pursuing
different aims, the poet's father a physician and his mother a grinder of grain.
One family, three trades, no hierarchy. A society with rigid hereditary
occupational castes does not produce that verse.

## The gift

It is tempting to say this dimension produced no gift at all. That would be
false, and the falsehood would be the comfortable kind.

The Vedic corpus gave social order a **language of interdependence**. The image
in 10.90 is a single body. Whatever hierarchy is implied by mouth and feet, the
hymn does not present four separate species — it presents four parts of one
organism, none of which survives alone. That image has been used by reformers
as often as by oppressors, and it is genuinely in the text.

Second, the corpus gave **an ideal of function over birth**, visible in the
earlier layers and never fully erased. The later tradition had to keep arguing
against the idea that worth follows from what one does, which tells us the idea
was there to argue with.

Third, and most consequentially: **it gave the evidence for its own
prosecution**. Everything in the previous section — the single verse, the
isolated word, the late layer, the modern diction — comes from within the
tradition's own preserved text and its own scholarship. The case against caste's
Vedic legitimacy is built entirely out of Vedic materials. Ambedkar could make
that case because the tradition had kept the evidence.

## The cost

The cost is the largest in this book, and I am not going to stage it as balance
against the gift. It is not balanced.

**A late, isolated verse was converted into a foundation.** Once 10.90.12 was
treated as the Veda's word on social order, the argument was over before it
began, because in a tradition that treats the Veda as beginningless and
authorless, a Vedic sanction is not a historical claim that can be dated and
disputed. It is simply how things are. The very reverence that preserved the
text so perfectly made the text impossible to argue with — and the thing made
unarguable was the one verse least representative of the corpus.

**The hierarchy hardened far past anything in the verse.** The verse names four
groups. It says nothing about untouchability, nothing about thousands of
endogamous sub-castes, nothing about barring people from wells or roads or
temples, nothing about hereditary handling of human waste. Every one of those
was built later, by people, and then attributed backwards. The Veda was made to
carry weight it never stated.

**The cost was paid, overwhelmingly, by those the verse placed at the feet.**
Denied literacy for generations, denied land, denied entry, denied the priestly
education that would have let them read the verse and notice how thin it was.
This is the cruellest mechanism in the whole story: **the exclusion was
self-sealing.** The people with the strongest reason to examine the text were
precisely the people forbidden to learn its language.

And it travelled. Indenture ships to Mauritius, Fiji, Trinidad, Guyana, Natal
and Suriname carried caste along with the people, and in many places it
reassembled itself within a generation or two — in a new hemisphere, under a
different colonial administration, with no Kuru polity anywhere in sight.

## What remains

The diaspora is where this dimension becomes genuinely interesting, because
caste did not survive the crossing intact.

On the plantations, the sheer mechanics of indenture broke enforcement: shared
barracks, shared labour, ships where everyone ate the same rations, sex ratios
so skewed that marrying within one's sub-caste was often simply impossible.
What emerged in Trinidad or Mauritius was flatter than what left — not from
reform, but from the impossibility of maintaining a structure that required
village-scale policing.

What persisted, with real force, was **endogamy in marriage** and a quiet
awareness of gradation. Anyone who has watched a matrimonial conversation in a
Gujarati family in Leicester or a Tamil family in Toronto knows the difference
between something being abolished and something going unspoken.

But the crossing produced something else. A generation raised outside the
enforcing structures, educated in the languages of their new countries, can go
to the text directly. And what they find, if they look at 10.90 with any care,
is a single late verse in a corpus of a thousand hymns, using a word that
appears nowhere else, in language a Victorian scholar could already tell was
younger than its neighbours.

Once someone has seen that, a specific question becomes unavoidable — the
question that runs under this entire book: **on whose authority was that one
verse made to mean so much?**

The Vedas do not answer. That is the answer. The authority was never theirs. It
was claimed by people who counted, correctly for a very long time, on no one
being permitted to check.
