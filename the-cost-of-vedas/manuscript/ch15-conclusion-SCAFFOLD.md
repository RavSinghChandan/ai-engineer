*My invented version:*

>

**Why this section exists:** this is the hinge of the whole book. The central
finding of the preceding fourteen chapters is that the largest costs did not
come from what the Vedas say. They came from what was attributed to the Vedas
by people who counted on no one checking. Caste hardened by citing a single
hymn — Rigveda 10.90 — that does not say what it was made to say. Women's
exclusion was justified by a corpus that names women as seers.

The authority was real. The claims made in its name often were not.

If you have your own version of discovering that gap, it is worth more to a
reader than any argument I can construct, because it is the experience the book
is trying to describe.

## 3. What it gave me

`[[FILL — THE DEBT]]`
*My invented version:*

> `[[SOMETHING CONCRETE — a habit of mind, a discipline, a way of sitting with a
> question, a practice you keep, a phrase you return to, a calm you can reach
> that you do not think you would have found elsewhere]]`

**The argument I can make on your behalf, which you may keep or cut:**

Three things in this corpus seem to me genuinely rare in the ancient world, and
I do not think I am being sentimental about them.

The first is **the licence to ask without a floor**. Rigveda 10.129, the
Nāsadīya, reaches the origin of everything and then refuses to claim knowledge
of it — *"who then knows whence it has arisen?"* — and ends by wondering whether
even the highest witness knows. A tradition that placed *that* in its most
protected text licensed doubt from the inside, permanently. No later authority
could call questioning foreign to the tradition, because questioning was in the
foundation.

The second is **transmission as an act of care**. This corpus survived for
millennia in human memory, with redundant checking methods built to catch a
single dropped syllable, before it was ever written down. Whatever one thinks
of the contents, that is one of the great sustained acts of collective
discipline in human history — and the people who did it were not paid, famous,
or thanked.

The third is **a vocabulary for the inner life** precise enough that it is still
being borrowed, often without acknowledgement, by people worldwide who would
not describe themselves as religious at all.

## 5. Why I still say uplifted

**The argument, for you to accept, sharpen, or reject:**

A reader who has come this far could fairly ask why a book that catalogues this
much damage ends on the word *uplifted*.

Here is my answer. Almost every instrument used to dismantle the worst of what
was built in the Vedas' name was itself drawn from the Vedas. The reformers who
fought untouchability quoted the texts. Those who fought for women's education
pointed to the women named as seers. Those who argued against ritual as
transaction cited the Upanishadic turn inward, which is itself the Vedic
corpus completing an argument with its own earlier layers.

That is not a coincidence, and it is not apologetics. It is a property of the
thing itself. **A tradition that preserves its own contradictions hands every
future generation the tools to correct it.** A tradition that had been tidied
into consistency — the dissent edited out, the women's names quietly reassigned,
the doubting hymn dropped — would have been far more dangerous, because there
would have been nothing inside it to appeal to.

The four Vedas are not a rulebook. They are an argument that has been running
for three thousand years and has never been closed. The cost in the title of
this book is real, and I have not discounted it in any of the fourteen chapters.
But the cost was extracted mostly by those who wanted the argument closed and
their own answer installed as the final one.

The Vedas themselves never did that. That refusal is the inheritance. It is why
I can write a book this critical of what was done in their name and still mean
it when I say they lifted us.
