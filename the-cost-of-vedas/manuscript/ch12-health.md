# Chapter 12 — Health and Medicine

## The bottle on the counter

It promises immunity. It cites a tradition five thousand years old. It costs
about the same as a week of the generic metformin that the same customer is
also buying, and the shelf it sits on is at eye level while the metformin is
behind the counter.

During the COVID-19 pandemic, products of this kind sold in enormous volume
across India and the diaspora. Some contained genuinely studied compounds. Some
contained nothing that has ever been tested. The packaging was frequently
indistinguishable.

That indistinguishability is the inheritance this chapter is about, and its
roots go back to the Atharvaveda.

## What the texts say

The Vedic corpus is not a medical literature, with one significant exception.

Three of the four collections concern ritual, liturgy and chant. The
**Atharvaveda** is different: it contains substantial material on healing —
fevers, wounds, poisons, parasites, childbirth, skin disease, hair loss, the
behaviour of the body in health and failure. It is the closest thing in the
corpus to a practical medical text.

It is also, in the same hymns, a book of charms. A remedy involving an
identifiable plant sits beside an incantation against a demon understood to
cause the fever. **The text draws no line between them**, because no line
existed to draw. Empirical observation and magical causation were a single
category.

The developed medical system, **Āyurveda**, is later. Its principal texts — the
*Caraka Saṃhitā* and the *Suśruta Saṃhitā* — are usually placed in the centuries
around the turn of the common era, roughly a thousand years or more after the
Vedic hymns. They are affiliated to the Atharvaveda by tradition and are
genuinely impressive documents: systematic, observational, organised around a
theory of bodily humours, and containing detailed surgical material.

The claim that Āyurveda is five thousand years old is marketing. The claim that
it descends from Vedic material is defensible. Those are different claims and
this book will not blur them.

Suśruta's surgical material deserves specific mention because it is genuinely
remarkable: descriptions of instruments, of cataract couching, of wound
management, and of a nasal reconstruction technique using a forehead flap. That
last is not a curiosity — when European surgeons encountered the Indian method
in the late eighteenth century it entered Western practice, and the forehead
flap remains in the reconstructive repertoire today.

## The gift

**A systematic, observational medicine, early.** Whatever its theoretical
framework, Āyurveda proceeded by classification, case observation and
documented practice. It produced a large materia medica, a surgical tradition,
and a coherent training structure at a time when most of the world had none.

**Contributions that survived translation into modern practice.** The
forehead-flap rhinoplasty is the clearest case. Several plant compounds in the
pharmacopoeia have identifiable pharmacological activity and are under
legitimate study. A tradition that catalogued thousands of plants over two
millennia generated a candidate library of real value.

**Prevention as the centre of the system.** Āyurveda's emphasis on daily
routine, diet, sleep and seasonal adjustment — *dinacaryā*, *ṛtucaryā* — treats
health as something maintained rather than restored. Modern medicine has been
arguing its way back toward this for fifty years and still struggles to deliver
it, because prevention has no billing code.

**The whole-person frame.** Treating the patient's constitution, circumstances
and mind as part of the clinical picture is not superstition; it is what
"biopsychosocial" means, and mainstream medicine adopted it late and
incompletely.

## The cost

**The inability to discard is the fundamental cost.** A system that cannot
distinguish its working remedies from its ineffective ones cannot improve. It
can only accumulate. Without controlled comparison — which no tradition
anywhere had before the modern era — there is no mechanism for removing what
does not work, and everything is preserved with equal authority. Chapter 8's
magnificent preservation apparatus, applied to medicine, guarantees that the
errors are transmitted as faithfully as the successes.

**Antiquity is being used as an evidence substitute right now.** *This is five
thousand years old, therefore it works* is not an argument, and it is the
central marketing claim of a large and growing industry. The Indian regulatory
framework permits traditional medicines to be sold with substantially lighter
evidentiary requirements than pharmaceuticals, and the result is a market where
tested and untested products share a shelf and a vocabulary.

**Heavy metals are a documented, ongoing harm.** *Rasa śāstra* preparations
intentionally incorporate mercury, lead and arsenic. Testing of Ayurvedic
products sold in the United States and India has repeatedly found toxic levels
of lead and mercury in a meaningful fraction of samples, and cases of heavy
metal poisoning traced to these products appear in the medical literature. This
is not a hostile characterisation; it is a measured finding, repeatedly
replicated.

**Delayed treatment kills people.** The most serious cost is the patient with a
treatable cancer, a treatable infection, or a treatable psychiatric illness who
spends six months on a traditional protocol first. This happens, it is
documented, and the tradition's prestige is what makes it possible.

**And legitimate research is contaminated by the claims made around it.** There
are genuinely promising compounds in this pharmacopoeia. Serious work on them is
made harder by an environment in which every finding is immediately overstated,
because the overstatement makes scientists reluctant to be associated with the
field at all.

## What remains

The diaspora holds this dimension in a split that most families would recognise
immediately: **allopathy for the emergency, tradition for the chronic and the
unexplained.**

The pattern is consistent. Nobody takes a fracture to an Ayurvedic practitioner.
But joint pain, digestion, fatigue, sleep, the aftermath of an illness with no
clean diagnosis — these go to turmeric, to ashwagandha, to a practitioner in
Wembley or Jackson Heights, often alongside a prescription the GP does not know
about. That last part is the clinical risk: interaction effects with
undisclosed supplements are a real and under-reported problem in South Asian
patient populations.

In the West, the same material has been rebranded as wellness and detached from
its origins entirely: turmeric lattes, ashwagandha gummies, "Ayurvedic" spa
treatments with no relationship to the textual tradition. The extraction is
thorough and the attribution is minimal.

The honest position is uncomfortable for everyone. The tradition produced real
medical knowledge, some of which modern medicine adopted and some of which is
still worth investigating properly. It also produced a large body of
ineffective and occasionally poisonous practice, which it had no mechanism to
identify or discard, and which is now sold at scale on the authority of its
age.

Both things are true. The bottle on the counter is the product of a system that
never had to tell them apart — and of a market that profits from the fact that
it still doesn't.
