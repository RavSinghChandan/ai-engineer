# Chapter 5 — Family and Relationships

## The WhatsApp group

There are forty-one people in it. It is called *Family*. It includes three
uncles she has met twice, a cousin's wife she has never met, and her father,
who forwards things.

When her marriage began failing, she did not post in it. She told one friend,
in another city, over a phone call she made from her car.

Forty-one people, and nowhere to put the truth. That gap — between the density
of obligation and the scarcity of intimacy — is where this chapter lives.

## What the texts say

The Vedic material treats the household as a **ritual unit**, and that single
fact explains most of what follows.

The domestic fire is the centre. The *gṛhastha*, the householder, is the person
who maintains it, and the rites of the household — birth, initiation, marriage,
death — are his obligation and his wife's jointly. A Vedic marriage is not
primarily a contract between two people or a union of two hearts. It is the
constitution of a unit capable of performing ritual, which neither person can
perform alone.

**Rigveda 10.85**, the Sūryā hymn, is the source text for the wedding and is
still recited at Hindu marriages today. Its language is worth reading directly
rather than through commentary. It addresses the bride as entering the
household to be its mistress; it speaks of the couple as a pair whose union is
modelled on cosmic order; it asks for prosperity, offspring and long
companionship. It is warm, and it is explicitly about function.

The *saptapadī* — the seven steps — is the binding act. Not the exchange of
vows, not consent expressed before a witness, but seven steps taken together,
after which the marriage is complete. The mechanism is shared movement, not
declared intention.

And the ancestors are present. The *pitṛ* rites make the dead dependent on the
living: offerings sustain them, and neglect leaves them unsustained. This binds
a family vertically across generations, in both directions — the living owe the
dead, and the unborn will owe the living.

What the Saṃhitās do **not** contain is the elaborate apparatus that later
governed Indian family life: the detailed rules on widow remarriage, the
specific prohibitions on inter-caste union, the developed joint-family property
law. Those are Dharmaśāstra material, centuries later. The distinction matters
in this chapter more than almost anywhere else in the book, because the
Dharmaśāstra rules are routinely defended as Vedic and are not.

## The gift

**A structure that made isolation nearly impossible.** The Vedic household is a
dense web of obligation, and the correlate of obligation is presence. An old
person is not alone. A bereaved person is not left to manage. A new mother is
surrounded, sometimes oppressively, by people whose role in that moment is
defined. Loneliness as a public health category — with measurable effects on
mortality — is a problem this structure largely solved, and solved without
professionals or funding.

**Marriage as a joint enterprise rather than a feeling.** Grounding a marriage
in shared function rather than in sustained emotional intensity is unfashionable
and unusually durable. It survives the periods when the feeling is absent,
because the feeling was never load-bearing. Whatever one concludes about arranged
marriage, this design feature is real and modern relationship research has been
circling back toward it.

**Continuity as a source of meaning.** A person who understands themselves as one
link between ancestors they owe and descendants who will owe them has an answer
to mortality that requires no metaphysics. The *pitṛ* rites are, functionally, a
technology for making individual death bearable by making the individual
non-central.

**Care for elders as default rather than decision.** The joint family absorbed
ageing without institutions. The cost of that absorption was paid almost
entirely by women, which the next section addresses — but the outcome, for the
elderly themselves, was better than what has replaced it in most places.

## The cost

**The obligation-to-intimacy ratio is brutal.** Forty-one people in the group,
and nowhere to say the true thing. A structure organised around duty and role
supplies enormous quantities of presence and very little confidentiality.
Emotional disclosure within an extended family carries consequence: it becomes
news, it becomes leverage, it becomes the family's business. The woman in the
car is not failing her family. Her family has no slot for what she needs.

**The cost of the care was invoiced almost entirely to women.** Every elder
absorbed, every child raised, every ritual maintained, every guest fed — this
labour was real, unpaid, unremitting, and gendered. The daughter-in-law's
position in a joint household is the clearest case, and Chapter 6 takes it up in
full. Any honest accounting of this dimension's gift must record whose
unremunerated work bought it.

**Endogamy hardened into the enforcement arm of caste.** The family is where
caste is actually reproduced. Not in temples, not in texts, but in whom one is
permitted to marry. Marriage rules are the mechanism by which Chapter 7's
hierarchy persisted through every reform, every constitution, and every
migration. A family system organised around ritual purity of lineage is a
caste-maintenance machine regardless of anyone's intentions.

**Individual preference had nowhere to go.** A structure that derives its
strength from role assignment handles badly those who do not fit the roles: the
person who does not wish to marry, the couple who cannot have children, the gay
son, the daughter who wants out. The framework's answer to each has
historically been pressure, and the pressure has a documented body count in
"honour" killings and in suicide rates among young married women in India that
remain among the highest in the world.

## What remains

The diaspora has run this dimension's experiment for a century, and the result
is a genuine hybrid rather than a collapse.

What crossed the water intact: the density of obligation, the flow of
remittance, the expectation that an ageing parent joins the household, the
marriage as a family project rather than an individual one. Anyone who has seen
a Gujarati wedding in Leicester or a Tamil one in Toronto has seen the *saptapadī*
performed exactly as the text specifies, in a hired hall, with the fire in a
metal container and a fire-alarm engineer on standby.

What did not cross: the enforcement. A joint family in Edison cannot police a
son who lives in Seattle. The village that once supplied both the support and
the surveillance supplied neither after the crossing — and the two turn out to
have been the same apparatus.

What emerged is a version of this structure that many diaspora families
describe, with some accuracy, as the best available: the obligation retained by
choice rather than by compulsion, the elders cared for, the marriage still a
family matter — and the exit available. The exit changes everything, because
an obligation one could leave and does not leave is a different thing from an
obligation one cannot leave.

The forty-one people in the group are still there. What is new is that she can
leave the group. She has not. That is the whole difference, and it is the best
outcome this dimension has produced in three thousand years.
