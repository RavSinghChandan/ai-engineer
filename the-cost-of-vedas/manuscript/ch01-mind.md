# Chapter 1 — Mind and Psychology

## Four hours, thirty-eight minutes

That is the average daily screen time of an Indian smartphone user, and it is
rising. The number is not the interesting part. The interesting part is what
sits underneath it: a person who cannot hold a single thought for the length of
a paragraph, who reaches for the phone before the thought that prompted the
reach has finished forming, and who describes this to a doctor as anxiety.

There is no Vedic word for *notification*. But there is a great deal of Vedic
material on a mind that will not stay where it is put, because that turned out
to be one of the oldest problems humans have written down.

## What the texts say

The Vedic corpus approaches the mind from a direction that still feels unusual:
**as something to be trained rather than expressed.**

The ritual hymns assume a practitioner capable of sustained, precise attention
under pressure. A Vedic recitation is not a performance to be felt; it is an
act of exactness, where a mispronounced syllable invalidates the rite. Whatever
one thinks of the theology, the training regime is real, and its target is
**attentional control**.

The most important single text here is the *Gāyatrī*, **Rigveda 3.62.10** — the
verse recited more times by more people than almost any other sentence in human
history. Its content is worth reading rather than assuming: it asks that the
mind be *impelled*, *directed*, *set in motion* toward understanding. It is a
petition about cognition. The most repeated verse in the tradition is a request
for a better-functioning mind.

The Upaniṣadic layer goes further and does something modern psychology took
until the twentieth century to formalise: **it separates the observer from the
observed within a single person.** The self that watches the mind is
distinguished from the mind being watched. Thoughts become objects in a field
of awareness rather than the self itself.

That move — *I am having an anxious thought* rather than *I am anxious* — is the
mechanism at the centre of cognitive behavioural therapy and of every
mindfulness-based clinical protocol now in use. It was set out, in substance,
about two and a half thousand years before it was operationalised in a clinic.

One limit must be marked. Detailed meditative technique — postures, breath
counts, staged absorption — belongs mostly to **later** material: the Yoga
Sūtras (c. 2nd–4th century CE), the Buddhist Abhidhamma, the Hatha texts. The
Vedic Saṃhitās supply the *goal* and the *model of mind*, not the manual. When
a wellness brochure promises "5,000-year-old Vedic meditation techniques," the
number is doing marketing work, not historical work, and this book will not
repeat it.

## The gift

**A model of mind that survives contact with modern evidence.** The
observer/observed distinction is not a metaphor that happens to sound
scientific; it is a functional description that produces measurable results.
Mindfulness-based cognitive therapy is now recommended in clinical guidelines
for preventing depressive relapse. The pipeline from Upaniṣadic introspection
to an NHS-commissioned course is real, if largely unacknowledged at the
terminus.

**A technology of attention, not merely a theory of it.** Repetition of a fixed
sound for a fixed count, at a fixed time, in a fixed posture, daily, for years.
Strip the metaphysics and it is a structured attention-training protocol with
extraordinary adherence — which is the hardest variable in any behavioural
intervention. Most modern attention programmes fail not because the exercise is
wrong but because nobody does it twice. This one embedded itself in a daily
ritual obligation and ran for three millennia.

**Permission to find the mind unreliable.** The tradition treats a wandering,
grasping, restless mind as the ordinary human condition rather than a personal
defect. For a reader currently interpreting their own distractibility as moral
failure, that reframing is worth more than most of what they will be sold.

**Vocabulary with no English equivalent.** *Citta*, *manas*, *buddhi*,
*ahaṃkāra* — distinctions between the mental field, the processing faculty, the
discriminating intellect, and the I-making function. English collapses all four
into "mind" and then struggles to describe what is happening in a person.

## The cost

**Interiority became an alternative to treatment.** This is the gravest cost in
this chapter, and it is being paid in India right now. A person with
melancholic depression, with psychosis, with bipolar disorder, is told the
problem is spiritual — insufficient practice, unresolved *karma*, weak
discipline. Treatment is delayed by years. India's treatment gap for severe
mental illness runs high by any measure, and while poverty and psychiatrist
shortage explain most of it, the framing that inner disturbance is a
self-correction problem does real, countable damage.

**The self-blame loop.** If the tradition says a trained mind is achievable, and
your mind is not trained, the fault is yours. Add *karma* and it is not merely
this life's fault. The tradition that generously described the wandering mind as
ordinary was also used to tell people their suffering was earned.

**Suppression sold as equanimity.** *Detachment* is an unusually easy word to
misuse. Practised, it means holding experience without being commanded by it.
Misused, it means not feeling things, and it has been deployed for generations
to tell grieving people to stop grieving, angry people that their anger is
spiritual immaturity, and abused people that their distress is attachment. What
the texts describe as a hard-won capacity was reissued as an instruction.

**And the commercial capture is nearly total.** The most sophisticated
psychological material in the corpus now reaches most people through a
subscription app, an eight-week retreat priced for foreign currency, or a
guru-brand with a merchandise line. The rigour is gone; the vocabulary is a
premium. Readers encountering *dhyāna* first as a paid tier will reasonably
conclude the tradition was always selling something.

## What remains

This dimension has travelled further than any other in this book, and travelled
mostly without its name attached.

Mindfulness-based interventions are in clinical guidelines. Attention research
cites contemplative traditions. Corporate wellbeing programmes run breathing
protocols in Bengaluru, Berlin and Boston. The Upaniṣadic move — watch the
thought instead of being it — is now ambient in global psychological culture.

What is striking, in the diaspora especially, is the route it took. A
second-generation Indian professional in Toronto or Reading frequently meets
this material **in English, through a therapist or an app**, and only afterwards
discovers it came from the tradition their grandmother practised. The
inheritance returned to them stripped of context, rebranded, and priced — and
they often find it more usable in that form, because it arrived without the
obligation, the guilt, and the family expectation attached.

That is a genuine gain and a genuine loss in the same motion, and I do not
think it resolves. What was preserved was the technique. What was dropped was
the framework that told you what the technique was *for* — and the framework
was the part that answered the question our four-hours-thirty-eight-minutes
reader is actually asking, which is not *how do I focus better* but *what should
I be paying attention to.*

The Vedas had an answer to the second question. The app has an answer to the
first. Most people now have the app.
