# Chapter 10 — Geography and Migration

## The water that should have stopped them

There is a rule in the Dharmaśāstra literature against crossing the sea.
*Samudrayāna* — ocean voyage — carried loss of caste. Cross the black water and
the ritual status that organised your entire life was gone.

Between roughly 1834 and 1917, more than a million people crossed it anyway, on
indenture contracts, to Mauritius, British Guiana, Trinidad, Natal, Fiji,
Suriname and beyond. Millions more followed under other arrangements in the
century after.

Today there are somewhere around thirty-five million people of Indian origin
living outside India — one of the largest diasporas on earth.

The rule lost. What is interesting is exactly which parts of the inheritance
crossed with them, which parts drowned, and why the survivors were not the ones
anyone would have predicted.

## What the texts say

Here is something that should be said early, because it cuts against the way
this dimension is usually discussed: **the sea-crossing prohibition is not
Vedic.**

The Rigvedic hymns are not the product of a people anxious about leaving. They
are full of movement — journeys, rivers, chariots, cattle-raids, routes. The
geography they describe is the northwest of the subcontinent and the river
system there, and the corpus's own *Nadīstuti*, the hymn in praise of rivers at
**Rigveda 10.75**, names rivers in sequence like a route rather than a boundary.

The land is treated as sacred, certainly. But sacredness in the early material
attaches to rivers, mountains and the fire that can be kindled anywhere — not to
a bounded territory one forfeits by leaving.

The prohibition on sea travel appears in the later legal literature, along with
much else in Chapter 7's apparatus, and it does the same work: it converts a
matter of practice into a matter of status, enforceable by the community that
stays behind.

This is a pattern worth naming, because it recurs in every chapter of this
book. **The restriction is late. The thing restricted is not.**

## The gift

**A portable religion.** This is the decisive inheritance for this dimension.
The Vedic ritual core requires fire, water, a few materials and a person who
knows the words. It does not require a building, a hierarchy, a pilgrimage
site, or a licensed institution. A family in a Fiji cane field could constitute
a household fire. A family in a Leicester terrace can constitute one now.
Traditions that require a consecrated building and an ordained officiant do not
travel like this.

**Memory as luggage.** Chapter 8's transmission system meant the tradition was
already stored in people rather than in objects. Indenture ships carried almost
nothing. The *Rāmcaritmānas* and the domestic rites crossed in heads. A
text-dependent tradition would have arrived damaged; this one arrived intact
because it had never been dependent on paper.

**A framework for holding two places at once.** The tradition's sense of the
sacred is distributive — a river here can be addressed as the Ganga, a fire here
is the same fire. This is why diaspora communities could sacralise new
geography rather than merely mourning the old. The Ganges water poured into a
Trinidad river is not a compromise within this framework; it is how the
framework works.

**Ritual as social infrastructure.** In places where indentured labourers had
nothing — no property, no legal standing, no institutions — the ritual calendar
supplied structure, assembly, mutual obligation and the basis for collective
action. Communities rebuilt around it because it was the only thing they had
been permitted to bring.

## The cost

**The crossing was frequently a deception, and the tradition's own rule
worsened it.** Indenture recruitment involved systematic misrepresentation of
terms, destinations and duration. The *samudrayāna* prohibition meant many who
went could not return without losing status — so the rule designed to prevent
departure functioned mainly to prevent return. It trapped people.

**Caste crossed the water.** Chapter 7's hierarchy reassembled in the colonies
within a generation or two, with no Kuru polity, no village, and no enforcement
apparatus — just people rebuilding the structure they knew. In East Africa,
Britain, Trinidad and Fiji, endogamy and gradation persist. The UK's own
equalities legislation has had to consider caste discrimination as a live
category in the twenty-first century.

**What arrived was frozen.** Diaspora practice frequently preserves the forms of
the emigration period while India itself moves on. Communities can be, in
specific respects, more conservative than the society they left — because the
tradition became the identity marker in a place where identity was under
pressure, and identity markers resist revision.

**And the loss runs on a generational clock.** The language goes first
(Chapter 9), then the meaning of the rites, then the rites themselves. What is
left by the third or fourth generation is often food, festival and marriage
preference — the tradition reduced to cuisine and endogamy, which is to say
reduced to its most decorative and its most exclusionary components
simultaneously.

## What remains

The most consequential fact in this dimension is that **the enforcement did not
cross the water and the text did.**

Every restriction examined in this book required a village: someone to observe
the violation, a community to impose the cost, and no exit. Indenture destroyed
all three. Shared barracks, shared rations, skewed sex ratios that made caste
endogamy literally impossible for many, and a plantation manager indifferent to
ritual status. The structure that had held for two and a half thousand years
was not reformed. It was rendered unenforceable.

What emerged was flatter. Not equal — Trinidad and Mauritius both carry caste
awareness into the present — but flatter than what left, and flatter than what
remained behind in the villages of Bihar and eastern Uttar Pradesh that the
emigrants came from.

And something the tradition never anticipated followed. A population with the
inheritance but without the enforcement, educated in other languages, can go to
the texts directly. They can read Chapter 7's single verse and count the hymns
around it. They can find that the sea-crossing rule that governed their
great-grandparents' lives is nowhere in the oldest material.

The rule said they would lose the tradition by leaving. What they lost by
leaving was the apparatus that had been imposed on the tradition. The tradition
itself arrived, in memory, in the only luggage indenture allowed — and in
several respects it arrived cleaner than it left.
