# Chapter 13 — Ethics and Conflict

## When both answers are wrong

A manager discovers that the firm's most senior engineer — a friend, a mentor,
the person who hired her — has been quietly falsifying test results for two
years. Reporting him destroys him and probably the division. Not reporting him
means shipping something that will eventually hurt someone.

There is no rule that resolves this. Every framework she has been given supplies
a principle that another principle contradicts.

The Indian tradition has a word for exactly this situation, and the fact that it
needed one is the most interesting thing about it.

## What the texts say

The central ethical term in this tradition is *dharma*, and it does not mean
what English-language summaries usually say it means.

It is not "religion," and it is not "duty" in the sense of a rule one obeys. It
is closer to **what upholds** — the conduct that maintains order, which varies
by person, role, situation and time. In the Vedic material it is bound to *ṛta*,
the cosmic order of Chapter 11: right action is action that keeps the system
coherent.

This is a **situational** ethic rather than a universal one, and the difference
is the whole chapter. A commandment applies identically to everyone. *Dharma*
does not. What upholds order for one person in one role at one moment may not
for another.

The tradition then names the failure case directly: *dharma-saṅkaṭa*, the
crisis in which duties genuinely conflict and no available action satisfies all
of them. The Mahābhārata is largely an extended examination of these, and its
most sustained treatment — the Bhagavad Gītā — takes as its premise a man
paralysed because two obligations he cannot abandon point in opposite
directions.

It must be said clearly that these are **post-Vedic** texts. The epics and the
Gītā are later by centuries. What the Vedic Saṃhitās supply is *ṛta* and the
idea of order-maintaining conduct; the developed ethical machinery is
downstream.

The corpus also contains, without embarrassment, a great deal of **sanctioned
violence**. The hymns celebrate Indra's destruction of enemies and fortresses.
Animal sacrifice is central to the ritual system. Later material develops the
warrior's duty to fight. *Ahiṃsā* — non-harm — becomes central to Indian ethics
largely through the Jain and Buddhist traditions and the reform movements they
influenced, not from the Vedic hymns, which are not pacifist documents.

## The gift

**An ethic that can handle context.** Rule-based systems break on hard cases
because rules cannot anticipate every situation, and a framework that tells the
manager "always report misconduct" has not engaged with her problem. A framework
that asks what her role, her relationships and her circumstances actually
require is doing more honest work.

**A vocabulary for moral conflict.** *Dharma-saṅkaṭa* names the situation in
which every option is a failure. This is enormously useful. A person who
believes a correct answer must exist and cannot find it concludes they are
inadequate. A person who knows that genuine dilemmas exist can choose, act, and
carry the cost — which is what the Gītā actually counsels.

**Moral seriousness about consequences one cannot avoid.** The tradition does
not offer absolution. It does not promise that right action feels right. The
Mahābhārata's victors are not happy, and the text does not pretend otherwise.
That is unusual, and it is mature.

**Debate as ethical method.** The argumentative tradition of Chapter 14 —
opponents stated at full strength, then answered — applies here too. Moral
positions were worked out by contest rather than by decree.

## The cost

**A situational ethic bends toward whoever is defining the situation.** This is
the structural weakness and it is severe. If right action depends on role, and
roles are assigned by the hierarchy of Chapter 7, then *dharma* delivers
different moral entitlements by birth. It becomes an instrument for encoding
inequality as ethics — and it was used exactly this way for two and a half
thousand years.

**The absence of universal prohibitions.** A framework without "this is wrong
for everyone always" is poorly equipped to say that untouchability is wrong,
that a war is wrong, that a practice is wrong regardless of who is doing it and
what their role requires. The reform movements that eventually said those things
generally had to import universalist commitments from elsewhere or construct them
from the tradition's own earlier layers against its later ones.

**Duty was used to override conscience.** *Do your duty without attachment to
outcome* can be, and has been, deployed to tell people to carry out orders they
believe are wrong. The formulation is doing a lot of work for whoever issues the
orders, and it has been invoked to justify both violence and passivity.

**And violence has sanction available in the corpus.** Any honest account has to
record that the tradition contains warrior ethics and sanctified conflict, and
that these have been drawn on in the modern period to authorise political
violence. The same corpus that supports *ahiṃsā* readings supports these. The
selective-quotation problem of Chapter 6 is at its most dangerous here.

## What remains

The tradition's most famous modern ethical export — Gandhian non-violent
resistance — is a synthesis rather than a retrieval, and saying so plainly
respects it more than the usual account does.

Gandhi drew on the Gītā, on Jain *ahiṃsā*, on Tolstoy, on Thoreau, on the Sermon
on the Mount. What he built was new. It worked, it changed the twentieth
century, and it was then re-exported to the American civil rights movement and
to resistance movements worldwide. It is the clearest case in this book of the
inheritance producing something of first-rank global value — by being combined
with material from outside itself.

In the diaspora, this dimension shows up most often as a negotiated ethics: a
second-generation professional operating in an institutional culture of explicit
universal rules, while carrying an inherited framework of situational and
relational obligation. Whether to hire the cousin. Whether family obligation
overrides a stated policy. Whether the rule or the relationship governs.

Both frameworks are coherent. They give different answers, and the person
holding both has to arbitrate without a procedure.

Which returns the manager to her problem. Her tradition will not tell her what
to do. What it will tell her — and this is not nothing — is that the absence of
a clean answer is not her failure. Some situations have no action that does not
damage something. The framework's counsel is to choose with clear eyes, act
without pretending the cost is not real, and not expect to feel absolved.

That is a harder inheritance than a rule. It is also, when the rules run out,
the only kind that is any use.
