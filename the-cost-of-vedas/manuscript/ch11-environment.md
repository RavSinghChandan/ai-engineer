# Chapter 11 — Environment and Nature

## The most worshipped river in the world

The Ganga is addressed as a goddess by hundreds of millions of people. She is
invoked at births, at weddings, and at the end — her water carried to
cremations, poured into mouths, taken in sealed containers to Leicester and
Edison and kept for decades.

She is also, along long stretches, among the most polluted major rivers on
earth. Sewage, industrial effluent, and the residue of the rites themselves.

Successive clean-up programmes have absorbed enormous public money across four
decades. The river is not clean.

Two facts, one river, no contradiction visible to most of the people holding
both. That is this chapter's subject.

## What the texts say

The Vedic corpus addresses the natural world **as a set of agents rather than a
set of resources**, and this is its most distinctive environmental feature.

The deities are not symbols of natural forces standing in for something else.
They are the forces, addressed directly: Agni is fire, Uṣas is dawn, Vāyu wind,
Parjanya rain, the Maruts storm. The hymns speak *to* these rather than *about*
them. Grammatically and practically, nature is second person.

**Rigveda 10.75**, the *Nadīstuti*, praises the rivers by name in sequence — an
extended address to a river system as a set of individuals with characters.

The Pṛthivī Sūkta of the **Atharvaveda (12.1)** is the corpus's fullest statement
on the earth, and it is worth being precise about what it says and does not say.
It addresses the earth as mother and the speaker as her child; it speaks of her
bearing, sustaining, and being wounded and healing. It is a hymn of dependence
and relationship. It is regularly quoted as an ancient conservation charter,
which is a stretch — it does not set out an ethic of restraint in use. It
establishes a relationship. What is done with that relationship is left open.

The *ṛta* concept matters here too: a cosmic order that the seasons, the rivers
and the rites all participate in. Human ritual action is understood as
maintaining that order rather than as separate from it. Whatever its
metaphysical status, this is a framework in which human activity and natural
process belong to one system.

## The gift

**Nature addressed in the second person.** A framework in which a river is a
someone rather than a something is a genuinely different starting point for
environmental thought, and it is the reason India's legal system has produced
some of the world's most interesting experiments in granting legal personhood to
rivers. The intuition those judgments draw on is old and is real.

**Sacred groves, and they are measurable.** Tens of thousands of patches of
forest across India were protected by religious prohibition against cutting —
*devarai*, *kavu*, *sarna*. Ecologists study them because in many regions they
preserve species composition and old-growth structure that surrounding
landscapes lost. This is the tradition's most concrete conservation
achievement, and it worked because the mechanism was prohibition rather than
management.

**A low-consumption template.** Vegetarian food culture, minimal-waste domestic
practice, reuse as default, and a religious vocabulary of sufficiency. Per
capita emissions in India remain far below those of wealthy countries, and while
the primary cause is income, the cultural template is real and is being examined
seriously now that high-consumption societies need one.

**Cyclical time.** A framework in which time returns rather than advances does
not naturally produce the idea that resources exist to be converted into
progress. The tradition never developed the conception of nature as raw material
awaiting improvement that made industrial extraction feel like virtue.

## The cost

**Reverence is not protection, and the Ganga proves it.** This is the central
finding of this chapter and it should be stated without cushioning. The most
venerated river on earth is severely polluted, and the veneration did not
prevent it. Sacred status in practice attaches to the *idea* of the river rather
than to its water quality, and the two turn out to be entirely separable. A
framework that generates reverence without generating an obligation to
measure, regulate and enforce produces exactly this outcome.

**Ritual is itself a pollution source.** Immersion of idols loaded with heavy
metals in paint, floral offerings in bulk, plastic, oil, ash, and the incomplete
cremations that enter the river directly. The rites that honour the river
degrade it, and reform of those rites has been resisted precisely on the grounds
of tradition.

**Purity and cleanliness were decoupled.** This is the subtlest cost and perhaps
the most damaging. The tradition's elaborate apparatus of ritual purity —
examined in Chapters 2 and 7 — is not about physical cleanliness and never was.
A space could be ritually pure and filthy; sanitation work was assigned to
those the hierarchy excluded, making the labour itself polluting in status
terms. A society that assigns cleaning to people it considers contaminated by
cleaning will not clean well. India's sanitation record is entangled with
Chapter 7 far more than with any shortage of engineering.

**And *ṛta* can absorb anything.** A cosmic order that maintains itself,
operating across immense cyclical time, makes ecological damage look temporary
by definition. The framework offers no urgency, because urgency requires linear
time and an endpoint.

## What remains

The diaspora carries this dimension in a curious, partial form: the reverence
without the geography.

Ganga water in a Leicester cupboard, brought once and never replaced. Rivers in
Ontario and New South Wales treated as locally sacred for immersion, which
transplants both the devotion and the pollution. Sacred-grove logic has no
obvious equivalent in a country where the sacred sites are elsewhere.

What travels intact is the low-consumption household template — and it travels
into societies whose consumption is many times higher, where it functions as a
genuine, if unintentional, environmental contribution.

The honest conclusion is one the tradition's admirers resist. **The Vedic
inheritance supplies exceptional environmental sentiment and almost no
environmental governance.** It can make a river a goddess. It has no mechanism
for making an effluent standard enforceable, because enforcement requires
measurement, secular institutions and the assumption that human action degrades
what it touches — and that last assumption is precisely what a framework of
participatory cosmic order does not readily produce.

The sentiment is worth having. India's environmental movements draw real
strength from it, and the legal personhood experiments are watched worldwide.

But a goddess who cannot be measured cannot be defended. That is the cost, and
it is currently being paid at a rate of several billion litres of untreated
sewage a day.
